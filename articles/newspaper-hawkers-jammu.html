Before Sunrise: The Lives of Newspaper Hawkers Who Wake Jammu Before Dawn

By Sahil Pandita

Editor's note: this feature was written as a practical writing exercise. The two quotes below are illustrative and attributed to composite/fictional names rather than named individuals interviewed for this piece — flagged here for transparency before publication.

JAMMU: Long before the first rays of sunlight touch the streets of Jammu and before tea stalls begin serving their first customers, hundreds of newspaper hawkers are already at work. Between 3:30 and 6:00 every morning, these unsung workers sort bundles of newspapers, organise delivery routes and race against time to ensure that the day's headlines reach homes, offices and shops before the city awakens.

Whether it is the bustling neighbourhoods of Gandhi Nagar, Talab Tillo, Jewel Chowk, Janipur, Bakhshi Nagar or the outskirts of Nagrota, newspaper hawkers form an invisible network connecting printing presses with thousands of readers every single day. Their work is often unnoticed, yet it remains an essential part of the newspaper industry.

The day begins well before dawn. Delivery vehicles carrying freshly printed newspapers arrive at distribution centers where bundles are unloaded and sorted according to publication and locality. Within minutes, hawkers collect their assigned copies, separate newspapers based on customer subscriptions and begin their delivery rounds on bicycles, motorcycles or on foot.

Every minute matters. Readers expect newspapers to arrive before they leave for work or begin their morning routine. Delays caused by heavy rain, fog, traffic disruptions or late printing schedules can affect hundreds of deliveries, making punctuality one of the most important aspects of the profession.

A Profession in Transition

The profession has witnessed remarkable changes over the years. There was a time when newspaper circulation steadily increased as families subscribed to multiple publications covering local, national and international affairs. Today, smartphones, digital news platforms and social media have transformed the way people consume information, reducing dependence on printed newspapers for breaking news.

Despite this digital shift, printed newspapers continue to retain a loyal readership. Many readers value the credibility, structured presentation and in-depth reporting offered by newspapers. Morning reading remains a daily habit for numerous households, businesses, educational institutions and government offices across Jammu.

For hawkers, however, declining circulation has introduced new challenges. Fewer subscriptions, rising fuel prices, increasing transportation costs and changing reading habits have affected their earnings. Some supplement their income by delivering magazines, milk packets or small parcels alongside newspapers, while others have taken up additional jobs after completing their early morning rounds.

"Earlier, almost every house subscribed to one or two newspapers. Now many people prefer reading news on their mobile phones. Still, there are loyal readers who wait for their newspaper every morning, and that keeps us going," says Ramesh Kumar, a composite representation of hawkers with over 25 years of experience.

Another composite voice, Mohammad Yousuf, reflects on the discipline the profession demands: "People rarely see us because we finish our work before sunrise. Delivering newspapers isn't just a job; it's a responsibility. Readers trust us to bring them the day's news before they step out of their homes."

A Trust Built Over Years

The relationship between newspaper hawkers and subscribers often extends beyond simple deliveries. Many develop long-standing personal connections with families they have served for years. They remember customers' preferences, manage subscription renewals and continue deliveries during festivals, holidays and adverse weather conditions. Their reliability has earned them the trust of generations of readers.

Experts believe newspaper hawkers remain an important link in the newspaper distribution ecosystem. Without their timely efforts, the printed newspaper would never reach its audience. Even as publishing houses invest in digital platforms, the physical newspaper still depends upon an efficient network of distributors and hawkers working together every morning.

As newspaper organizations continue adapting to digital journalism, the role of hawkers is evolving rather than disappearing completely. Media experts note that the newspaper distribution system is built upon teamwork involving reporters, photographers, editors, designers, printing staff, transport workers and finally the hawker who places the newspaper at the reader's doorstep. While journalists write the news and presses print it, hawkers complete the final and perhaps most visible stage of the process.

As Jammu awakens to another day, thousands of residents begin reading about politics, sports, business and local events without often thinking about the journey that newspaper has taken before reaching their homes. Their contribution may not always make headlines, but it ensures that headlines reach everyone else.
