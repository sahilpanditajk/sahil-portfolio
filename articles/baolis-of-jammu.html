Forgotten Baolis of Jammu Await Revival as Heritage Fades Amid Urban Expansion

By Sahil Pandita

An old baoli in Jammu reflects the region's rich architectural and water conservation heritage, standing as a reminder of traditional community water systems despite years of neglect. | Photo: Sahil Pandita

JAMMU: Hidden behind expanding residential colonies, overgrown vegetation and the rapid pace of urban development, several historic baolis or stepwells across Jammu continue to silently narrate stories of a bygone era. Once serving as vital sources of drinking water, resting places for travellers and centers of community life, many of these centuries-old structures are now struggling against neglect, encroachment and a lack of public awareness.

From the historic surroundings of Purmandal to the outskirts of Nagrota and several lesser-known locations around Jammu, these stone-built water structures reflect the region's architectural heritage and its traditional methods of water conservation. Though modern water supply systems have reduced their practical importance, historians and conservationists believe these monuments remain valuable reminders of Jammu's cultural identity and engineering excellence.

Unlike ordinary wells, baolis were carefully designed with descending stone steps leading to a water reservoir, allowing people to access water even during periods of low water levels. Their construction demonstrated remarkable engineering techniques that enabled communities to collect, store and preserve groundwater throughout the year. Built using locally available stone and traditional craftsmanship, many of these structures have withstood the test of time despite centuries of changing weather conditions and human activity.

More Than a Water Source

Historically, baolis were much more than water sources. They served as social and cultural gathering spaces where villagers met to exchange news, travellers rested during long journeys and women collected water for their households. Many stepwells were constructed near temples, shrines and ancient trade routes, making them an important part of everyday life. Religious ceremonies, community interactions and seasonal festivals often revolved around these water bodies, highlighting their significance beyond their practical purpose.

The architectural design of Jammu's historic baolis also reflects the artistic skills of local craftsmen. Intricately carved stone steps, arches and retaining walls showcase traditional construction techniques that balanced functionality with aesthetics. Some stepwells were designed with resting platforms and shaded areas, offering relief to travellers during the hot summer months.

A Slow Decline

Over the years, however, rapid urbanisation has significantly altered the landscape around many historic stepwells. Expansion of residential colonies, changing land use patterns and limited maintenance have contributed to their gradual deterioration. In several locations, vegetation has covered stone walls, while accumulated waste, damaged masonry and natural erosion have affected the appearance of these heritage structures.

The decline of these heritage sites also reflects changing lifestyles. With the availability of piped water supply, borewells and modern infrastructure, the practical necessity of community stepwells has diminished considerably. As daily dependence on these structures reduced, routine maintenance gradually disappeared, leaving many baolis vulnerable to neglect.

Experts on heritage conservation often point out that preserving historical monuments is not only about protecting old buildings but also about safeguarding the stories, traditions and collective memories associated with them. Stepwells represent an important chapter in the history of water management in the Indian subcontinent.

A Case for Revival

The conservation of baolis also has environmental importance. Traditional water harvesting systems are increasingly being recognised for their relevance in discussions surrounding sustainable water management and climate resilience. Conservation experts also believe that restored baolis could become important centres for heritage tourism in Jammu — visitors today increasingly seek destinations that provide authentic cultural experiences rather than conventional sightseeing alone.

Educational institutions and local communities can also play an important role in preserving these monuments. Heritage walks, awareness campaigns, student-led documentation projects and community cleanliness drives can encourage greater public participation. However, long-term conservation requires coordinated efforts involving government departments, archaeologists, historians, environmental experts, local residents and heritage organisations.

As Jammu continues to grow and modernise, preserving its historical landmarks becomes increasingly important. The city's identity is shaped not only by new infrastructure but also by monuments that connect present generations with their past. The stone steps of these forgotten baolis may no longer witness the daily crowds they once attracted, yet they continue to stand as enduring symbols of resilience, craftsmanship and sustainable living.
