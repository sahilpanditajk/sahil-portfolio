Silent Guardians of the Forest: How Gujjar and Bakarwal Communities Continue to Preserve Traditional Pastoral Life

By Sahil Pandita

Members of the Gujjar and Bakarwal communities continue their seasonal pastoral journey with livestock through the hills of Jammu, preserving a centuries-old nomadic tradition deeply connected to nature. | Photo: Sahil Pandita

JAMMU: As the first rays of sunlight touch the foothills of the Shivalik ranges, Gujjar and Bakarwal families begin another day of a lifestyle that has remained largely unchanged for generations. While cities wake to the sounds of traffic and urban life, these nomadic communities prepare their livestock for long journeys through forests and mountain trails, continuing a pastoral tradition that has shaped the cultural landscape of Jammu and Kashmir for centuries.

The Gujjar and Bakarwal communities are among the oldest pastoral groups in the Himalayan region. Known for their seasonal migration, they travel with their herds of sheep, goats, buffaloes and horses between the plains of Jammu during winter and the alpine meadows of Kashmir and higher Himalayan regions during summer. This traditional practice, known as transhumance, allows them to make sustainable use of natural grazing resources while maintaining a lifestyle closely connected to nature.

For generations, this migration has not only been an economic activity but also a way of life. Families move together, carrying household essentials and living in temporary shelters during their journey. Children often grow up learning traditional knowledge about forests, mountain routes, weather patterns and livestock care from their elders — skills that form an important part of the region's intangible cultural heritage.

Guardians of the Ecosystem

A shepherd watches over a grazing flock in the Himalayan foothills, part of the seasonal transhumance that has shaped the region for centuries. | Photo: Sahil Pandita

Beyond their economic contribution, these communities have long been recognised for their deep understanding of forests and natural ecosystems. Living in close harmony with nature has enabled them to develop traditional practices that promote sustainable grazing and responsible use of natural resources. Environmental experts increasingly acknowledge the value of this indigenous knowledge in biodiversity conservation — their seasonal movement helps prevent overgrazing in a single area while allowing natural vegetation to regenerate over time.

Contemporary Challenges

Despite their rich cultural heritage, the Gujjar and Bakarwal communities face several contemporary challenges. Expanding urbanisation, infrastructure development, changing land-use patterns and environmental changes have affected traditional migration routes in many areas. Access to education, healthcare and other public services also remains difficult for families that spend several months each year on the move.

Children belonging to nomadic families often encounter interruptions in their education because of seasonal migration. Although various initiatives have sought to improve educational access through mobile schools, seasonal learning centres and hostel facilities, ensuring uninterrupted education for migratory communities continues to require sustained attention.

At the same time, younger members of the community are increasingly balancing tradition with modern aspirations. Improved road connectivity, mobile communication and greater access to education have opened new opportunities — while some continue their ancestral occupation with pride, others pursue higher education, government services, entrepreneurship and professional careers without losing touch with their cultural identity.

A Living Heritage

Cultural traditions continue to remain an integral part of Gujjar and Bakarwal identity. Their language, folklore, music, traditional attire and customs reflect centuries of history deeply connected with the mountains and forests of Jammu and Kashmir. Seasonal migration itself represents a living cultural practice that has survived changing political, economic and environmental conditions.

As Jammu and Kashmir continues to modernise, the challenge lies in ensuring that development reaches these communities without weakening the traditions that define their identity. The forests and mountain trails of the region have witnessed the footsteps of Gujjar and Bakarwal families for generations — as silent guardians of the forests, they continue to preserve not only a traditional occupation but an invaluable cultural and ecological legacy that deserves greater recognition, respect and support.
